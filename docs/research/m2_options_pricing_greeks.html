1. Goal & Context

The objective of Moonshot M2 is to implement a fully differentiable options pricing and Greeks stack in PyTorch. This module will provide a foundational mathematical toolkit for derivative pricing, risk hedging, and volatility surface modeling, designed to integrate with the Heterogeneous Graph Neural Network (HetTGN) as downstream predictive features or direct portfolio optimization heads.

By leveraging PyTorch's autograd engine, we can compute sensitivity parameters (the Greeks) through backward propagation. This provides a seamless, unified interface where options prices and risk exposures are differentiable with respect to all underlying parameters (spot price, strike, time-to-maturity, risk-free rate, dividend yield, and implied volatility).

2. Mathematical Formulations & Lit Review

2.1. European Options: Black-Scholes-Merton (1973)

The analytical price of a European call \(C\) and put $P$ on an asset with spot price \(S\), strike \(K\), continuous risk-free rate \(r\), continuous dividend yield \(q\), time-to-expiration \(T\), and continuous volatility \(\sigma\) is:

\[d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\] \[d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\] \[C(S, K, T, r, q, \sigma) = S e^{-q T} N(d_1) - K e^{-r T} N(d_2)\] \[P(S, K, T, r, q, \sigma) = K e^{-r T} N(-d_2) - S e^{-q T} N(-d_1)\]

Where \(N(x)\) represents the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution: \[N(x) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ 1 + \text{erf}\left( \frac{x}{\sqrt{2}} \right) \right]\]

2.2. Differentiable Greeks

The sensitivity of the option price to its parameters is defined by the first- and second-order partial derivatives (Greeks). While closed-form formulas exist, automatic differentiation allows us to calculate them universally without hardcoding complex formulas:

Greek Definition Analytical Formula (European Call with Divs) PyTorch Autograd Implementation
Delta (\(\Delta\)) \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) \(e^{-q T} N(d_1)\) torch.autograd.grad(V.sum(), S, create_graph=True)[0]
Gamma (\(\Gamma\)) \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\) \(\frac{e^{-q T} n(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{T}}\) torch.autograd.grad(Delta.sum(), S, create_graph=True)[0]
Vega (\(\mathcal{V}\)) \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma}\) \(S e^{-q T} \sqrt{T} n(d_1)\) torch.autograd.grad(V.sum(), sigma, create_graph=True)[0]
Theta (\(\Theta\)) \(-\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\) \(-\frac{S e^{-q T} n(d_1) \sigma}{2 \sqrt{T}} + q S e^{-q T} N(d_1) - r K e^{-r T} N(d_2)\) -torch.autograd.grad(V.sum(), T, create_graph=True)[0]
Rho (\(\rho\)) \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\) \(K T e^{-r T} N(d_2)\) torch.autograd.grad(V.sum(), r, create_graph=True)[0]

Note: \(n(x)\) is the standard normal probability density function (PDF): \[n(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2 / 2}\]

2.3. Implied Volatility Solver (Newton-Raphson)

To find the implied volatility \(\sigma_{\text{impl}}\) such that \(V(\sigma_{\text{impl}}) = V_{\text{market}}\), we use the iterative Newton-Raphson method: \[\sigma_{n+1} = \sigma_n - \frac{V(\sigma_n) - V_{\text{market}}}{\text{Vega}(\sigma_n)}\] To make this fully differentiable and robust, we:

  • Execute a fixed number of iterations (e.g., 15) to maintain continuous gradients and vectorized operations on GPU.
  • Incorporate a fallback mechanism (e.g., Bisection or Halley updates) if Vega approaches zero.
  • Clamp volatility updates to a positive, numerically safe interval \([\epsilon, \sigma_{\max}]\) to prevent negative or exploding numbers.

2.4. American Options: Barone-Adesi Whaley (1987) Approximation

Unlike European options, American options can be exercised at any time. Barone-Adesi Whaley (BAW) provides a highly efficient, analytic approximation for pricing American options under the Black-Scholes framework. It decomposes the American option price into the European price plus an early exercise premium:

For a Call option with \(S < S^*\): \[C_{\text{Am}}(S) = C_{\text{Eur}}(S) + A_2 (S / S^*)^{q_2}\] Where \(S^*\) is the critical spot price at early exercise boundary, solved iteratively.

5. Risks & Mitigations

Technical Risk: Division by Zero & NaN gradients.
As \(T \to 0\) or \(\sigma \to 0\), \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) division terms will result in division by zero, leading to NaN values and destroying gradient tracking.
Mitigation: Enforce strict numerical bounds (e.g., clamp \(T = \max(T, 1e-5)\) and \(\sigma = \max(\sigma, 1e-4)\)) inside the math operations.
