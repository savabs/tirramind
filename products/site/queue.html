The AI power queue
is mostly vapour.

To connect a power plant or a big datacenter to the grid, you file a request with your region’s grid operator and wait in line. That line is the queue.

A gigawatt (GW) is 1,000 megawatts (MW), the size AI datacenter projects ask for. Texas has 466 GW in line, the Midwest 699 GW. Forecasters quote those totals as if everything in line were on its way.

I counted 10,996 requests that are already finished — built, or given up on. About one in five got built. At 1,000 MW and above, one in nineteen.

The four operators run the Midwest, New York, California and New England — MISO, NYISO, CAISO and ISO-NE. Texas is not among them, so the 466 GW above is not in these figures: it blocks automated downloads. Nor is the mid-Atlantic (PJM), which needs a free key, nor the central plains (SPP), which never answered.

Size is the whole story

Above 100 MW, each step up in size cuts the chance of being built: 23.9%, then 12.6%, then 5.3%. Under 100 MW the pattern breaks. The 20–100 MW band does worse than both of its neighbours.

The last column is the range the true rate is likely to sit in. It is drawn so that, on twenty sets of data like this, about nineteen of the ranges — 95% of them — would contain the true rate. Bands with fewer requests get wider ranges. These are Wilson intervals, named so you can recompute them: the ordinary textbook formula misbehaves for rates this close to zero, which is exactly where the big projects sit.

The AI datacenter story is entirely gigawatt-scale — the size class that, historically, almost never gets built.

Why it is a range, not one number

The headline is about 1 in 5. Here is the working behind it. I counted three ways, and they disagree.

14.2% counts the built ones across every request that has ended. That is too low. A request filed in 2024 has not had time to be built. Counting it now marks a project still waiting as a failure.

21.4% groups requests by the year they joined the line, and counts each year on its own. I only use a year once at least 90% of its requests have finished. That leaves 5,436 requests.

That is too high. 1,354 finished requests carry no filing date, so they drop out of every year group. Almost all are failures: 1,351 given up on, 3 built. Most of them were filed before 2000.

17.2% puts the dateless ones back in. So the answer sits between 17.2% and 21.4%. I show both ends. Showing one would hide one of the two flaws above.

Two bugs I hit

Mixed date formats silently destroyed 7,400 of 9,640 dates. Operators write filing dates four ways: 2003-11-18 08:00:00, 1/14/2025, 2025-10-08T00:37:52+00:00, 2008-01-30. The date tool (pandas.to_datetime) read the first, assumed the rest matched, and blanked the others. 84% vanished. A blanked date looks exactly like one nobody filled in. It surfaced only because the script checks whether requests with no date got built at a different rate.

Sloppy technology labels invented a finding. My first table read Solar 32.1% beside Photovoltaic 13.0%, and Wind 19.1% beside Wnd 26.9%. Each operator spells a technology its own way. The difference was not solar against photovoltaic. It only showed which operator had filed the row. Anyone who published that split would have published a mistake. So “Solar” and “Photovoltaic” became one label, and so did “Wind” and “Wnd”.

What this does not show

The technology split is not a fair fight. Regions build different things. Part of the difference between gas and solar is really about where the project is, not what it burns.

Coverage has holes. California’s report stops at March 2023. The Midwest queue begins in 2015. “Built” is generous. It means the paperwork with the operator finished, not that the plant switched on. No forecast. This counts what already happened. No model is fitted.

The two markets you most want are missing. Texas publishes requests from very large electricity users only as a lump total, and blocks software from downloading the detail. The mid-Atlantic operator makes you sign up for a free key. A third operator, in the central plains, timed out when I asked.

Check it yourself

The Midwest operator serves its whole queue — 3,828 requests with status, county, capacity and dates — from one open address. No sign-up:

https://www.misoenergy.org/api/giqueue/getprojects

Count the ones given up on. It takes about a minute. You do not have to take my word for any of the above. Your totals will not match mine: the operators overwrite this file, and nobody keeps the old value.

Why believe these numbers

The last time I measured my own work, it failed, and I said so. I built a tool on public financial data and tested whether it predicted anything. I tested fifty-one ideas. Test that many and some will look good on luck alone. So I raised the bar (the Benjamini–Hochberg correction, named so you can repeat it) a result had to clear before I would call it real. Zero of the fifty-one cleared it.

I withdrew the product. I published the empty result, with my workings on the strength of the test. The ranking I had been about to sell turned out to be 90% driven by a date stored as a plain number.
